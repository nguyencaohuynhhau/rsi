# ch03: War School to Waking Up in America (pp. 24–50)

## Question this chapter answers

How did Frank Sheeran’s prewar restlessness, prolonged combat, and postwar maladjustment form the psychological and practical skills that later made him useful as a mob enforcer—before he ever met Jimmy Hoffa?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Little Egypt University | p. 27 | Sheeran’s comic name for losing his virginity and learning sex from carnival dancers Little Egypt and Neptune of the Nile; marks sexual “education” as apprenticeship. |
| 411 combat days | p. 31 | Army figure Sheeran cites for his own front-line days in the 45th Infantry Division; basis for $20 extra monthly combat pay; far above the “average” ~80 combat days he attributes to most veterans. |
| Killer Division | p. 34 | Patton’s label for the 45th, as reported by officers; the unit was told it would see more combat than others and should refuse surrenders and kill prisoners. |
| Anzio Express | p. 39 | Nickname for a large German rail-mounted artillery piece that shelled the Anzio beachhead at night; emblem of demoralizing, impersonal death. |
| what I had to do | p. 41 | Sheeran’s euphemism for killing prisoners and other combat atrocities; later reused for mob killings (see ch. 10). |
| encased in lead | p. 44 | Sheeran’s image for the emotional hardening of long combat: loss of civilian “moral skill,” replacement by personal rules. |
| what will be will be | p. 44 | Postwar motto: birth and death dates fixed; fatalism that reduces fear of consequences. |
| stand-up | (implied through conduct; named more fully later) | Here shown in embryo: not ratting, following combat’s two hard rules, not deserting when returning to the line. |

## Leading propositions

- Childhood street fighting and father’s beer-bet matches produced “mental toughness” that later suited soldiers. (p. 24)
- Expulsion after decking the Darby High principal and refusing to be his father’s punching bag marks the end of childhood subordination. (p. 24)
- Carnival work (Little Egypt / Neptune) and Maine logging were itinerant apprenticeships in rootlessness, hard physical work, and fighting older men. (pp. 25–29)
- Brandt asserts that prolonged combat is where Sheeran “learned to kill in cold blood.” (p. 31)
- The 45th Infantry Division suffered 21,899 battle casualties and 511 days of combat as a unit; Sheeran’s 411 days were more than 80% of the division’s total. (pp. 32, 31)
- Patton explicitly instructed the 45th to kill surrendering enemies and minimize prisoners; Biscari prisoner massacres followed those instructions. (pp. 34–36)
- Sheeran claims he killed small groups of German prisoners (“I did what I had to do”) and participated in the killing of ~500 Dachau guards after liberation. (pp. 41, 42)
- Postwar, Sheeran lost civilian “moral skill,” slept only three or four hours a night, drank heavily, and flared into violence over trivial provocations. (pp. 43–45)
- Domestic settlement (Mary Leddy, daughters, Food Fair driving job) coexisted with petty theft (chickens with Dusty), bouncer scams, and the vow to make money for a growing Catholic family—the hinge into downtown and Bufalino. (pp. 47–50)

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (1) Sheeran already knew violence, self-reliance, and survival from Philly childhood; (2) he voluntarily left a noncombat MP billet for infantry action; (3) he endured extreme duration of combat under Patton’s “Killer Division” culture; (4) he describes prisoner killings and Dachau reprisals without later moral reframing. **Conclusion**: War did not invent Sheeran's capacity for violence so much as professionalize, desensitize, and moralize it as obedience and necessity—“doing what I had to do.”
- **Premises**: Brandt documents Patton’s orders and the Biscari courts-martial (Compton acquitted as following orders; West convicted then released); Sheeran’s oral account of prisoner handling matches that institutional climate. **Conclusion**: Cold-blooded killing of captives was, in Sheeran’s unit culture, framed as following orders rather than as freestanding murder—training that maps onto later mob “orders.”
- **Premises**: Homecoming brings nightmares, insomnia, alcohol, hair-trigger fights, and inability to take ordinary supervision; civilian life feels unreal (“waking up in America”). **Conclusion**: The enforcer temperament is forged not only in combat acts but in the failure to reintegrate—violence and hustling become how a “lead-encased” man functions.
- **Premises**: Steady Food Fair driving; chicken skim with Dusty under weight-substitution; Budd’s “butcher shop” safety horrors; later *On the Waterfront* ambition (stated as the chapter closes into downtown). **Conclusion**: By the mid-1950s Sheeran already has theft craft, union-adjacent work, and hunger for more—formation before Hoffa and before Bufalino’s full mentorship.

## Evidence offered

### Sheeran’s oral testimony (first person)
- Prewar biography: Darby High expulsion; Regent carnival with Yank Quinn; logging camp boxing; Pearlstein Glass apprenticeship; Harvest Moon Ball second place (1939) with Roseanne De Angelis. (pp. 24–30)
- Enlistment (Aug. 1941), MP at Lowry Field, failed airborne (dislocated shoulder), infantry private who left and returned as private; 50 days AWOL but never when unit returned to the line. (pp. 31–34)
- Combat path: Sicily → Salerno beachhead → Monte Cassino → Anzio (four months in dugouts) → Rome → Operation Dragoon / St. Tropez → Alsace-Lorraine → Harz Mountains → Bamberg, Nuremberg → Dachau → Munich. (pp. 36–42)
- Specific atrocity claims: handling “a handful of Germans” as prisoners in Alsace; German soldiers made to dig shallow graves in the Harz; Dachau guards “taken care of.” (pp. 41–42)
- Postwar jobs, kangaroo fight meeting Mary Leddy, 1947 marriage, disorderly-conduct/resisting-arrest case (Feb. 4, 1947), Food Fair driving and chicken skim. (pp. 45–50)

### Brandt’s documentary / secondary framing
- Difficulty eliciting combat memories: two years before Sheeran would discuss them. (p. 31)
- 45th Division official Combat Report: casualty and combat-day figures; quotes on Salerno and Anzio. (pp. 32, 38–40)
- Patton speeches via Colonel George E. Martin and other officers; Patton diary entry (July 15, 1943) on Biscari; courts-martial of Captain John T. Compton and Sergeant Horace T. West. (pp. 34–36)
- Hirschson / British newspaperman memo on further Sicilian atrocities and Patton’s “bloodthirsty” instructions taken “too literally” by the 45th. (p. 36)
- General Mark Clark on 72 continuous combat days before Anzio. (p. 38)
- Churchill on Anzio “disaster”; Combat Report on Dachau’s 1,000 bodies, gas chamber and crematorium. (pp. 39, 42)

### Scrutinizing the evidence
- **Combat-day figure**: 411 is presented as Army-told to Sheeran; Brandt cross-checks with division report (511 unit combat days) and finds Sheeran’s share extreme—but the precise personal count is still Sheeran’s report of what “the Army told me.”
- **Prisoner killings**: Sheeran is elliptical (“I did what I had to do”); he does not name dates, places, or body counts for his own prisoner killings the way official records name Biscari. The parallel to Patton’s orders is interpretive (Brandt) plus analogical (Sheeran).
- **Dachau**: Collective “us” and camp victims borrowing guns; no individual confession of a named shooting—shared unit responsibility narrative.
- **Postwar violence** (Tastykake rake fight, trolley brawl, kangaroo/referee fight): colorful oral history; the 1947 booking is the first “rap sheet” legal proceeding he cites.
- **Brandt’s thesis sentence** (“learned to kill in cold blood”) is authorial judgment, not a Sheeran self-diagnosis; Sheeran prefers necessity and fatalism language.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **From ch. 1–3 (punching bag / early Philly)**: Father’s violence and street fighting reappear as readiness for war; the principal’s jaw and refusal to fight the father close the childhood arc begun earlier.
- **Forward to Bufalino (ch. 8–10)**: War’s two iron rules (return for the line; obey combat orders) foreshadow mob “do what you gotta do”; “what I had to do” language returns almost verbatim when Angelo Bruno orders Whispers killed. Sicily/Catania becomes social capital with Sicilian-born Bufalino.
- **Forward to Hoffa / Teamsters**: *On the Waterfront* fantasy, Local 107 contact Joey McGreal (lottery), Food Fair union protection, and Budd Manufacturing’s unsafe shop all prime “union work” ambition.
- **Voice tension**: Brandt stresses cold blood and documentary atrocity; Sheeran stresses survival, orders, prayers at Anzio, and fatalism—less confession of sadism than of habituation.
- **Moral skill vs. stand-up**: Combat teaches flouting ordinary authority while obeying lethal orders—the same pattern as later loyalty (not ratting Food Fair co-conspirators) and hit contracts.

## Open questions

- How much of the prisoner-killing and Dachau narrative is unit common knowledge Sheeran adopts versus personal acts he performed?
- Does Brandt over-determine the “learned to kill in cold blood” thesis from duration of combat alone, given many veterans with fewer days and different outcomes?
- How reliable are Sheeran’s combat-day and “never hit by German bullet or shrapnel” claims without his individual service record presented in full?
- To what extent is postwar violence (jobs, trolley, kangaroo) causal preparation for contract killing versus ordinary postwar PTSD/alcohol patterns shared by non-mob veterans?
- When exactly does “downtown” attachment begin relative to the birth of the third daughter (1955) and the first Bufalino meeting—timeline compression in oral history?
